Paracord Bracelet

Carlee’s Creations · Author Carlee Burgess · Oct. 19, 2026

Cobra weave. One knot, then its mirror, tied over and over. Ten feet of cord turns into a bracelet that fits your wrist.

[Carlee: one sentence, in your own words, about why you like this bracelet]

Grown-up jobs first. Scissors are sharp, so cut away from your body. Melting the cord ends with a lighter is for an adult only. No adult handy? Use the glue finish in Step 7. A bracelet is a craft, not rescue gear. The “550” on the package is the cord’s minimum breaking strength under the old military spec. It does not rate a knotted bracelet or its buckle. Never trust one to hold a person, to climb, or to rappel.

What you need

  • 10 feet of 550 (Type III) paracord, one color. A 10-foot hank was listed around $8 to $9 in October 2026
  • One 3/8-inch side-release buckle. Listed from $0.99 each
  • Sharp scissors
  • Ruler or tape measure
  • For the ends: an adult with a lighter and a metal spoon, or fabric glue for a no-heat finish
  • Time: [Carlee: how long your first bracelet took]

Total for one bracelet: about $10. Prices change, so check the store.

Steps

  1. Measure. Wrap a piece of cord around your wrist, pinch the spot, and measure it on a ruler. A 7-inch wrist takes about 8 to 10 feet. The rule is about a foot of cord for each inch of bracelet.

  2. Fold, hitch, thread. Fold the cord in half. Push the fold through the top of one buckle half, bring the loop over the buckle, and pull it tight. That is a cow hitch. Now push both ends through the other buckle half. Don’t twist the two cords around each other.

  3. Set the length. Pull the loop to about wrist size and unclip the buckle. Measure from one buckle to the other, leaving out the buckle teeth. It should be 1 inch longer than your wrist. Lay it down with one long cord on each side and the two middle cords flat.

  4. The knot. Cross the left cord over the middle, leaving a loop on the left. Bring the right cord over the left cord, behind the middle cords, and up through the loop. Pull snug right under the buckle. Check that the length hasn’t changed.

    [Carlee: your own step photos go here]

  5. Mirror it. Tie the same knot backward. Right cord across the top, left cord over it and behind, up through the right loop. Pull snug. One of each makes one cobra stitch.

  6. Keep going. Alternate left and right. Every few knots, slide them together so the weave stays tight. Stop when the knots reach the other buckle.

  7. Finish. Cut each end about ¼ inch from the last knot. An adult melts each end until it turns glossy, then presses it flat against the bracelet with the back of a metal spoon, not a finger. No heat? Tuck the ends back through the weave and add a dab of fabric glue.

Fun fact

Paracord is short for parachute cord. It began as the lines that tie a parachute’s canopy to the person underneath. After World War II, surplus parachute cord reached everyday people, and crafters have been tying it into bracelets, key chains, and belts ever since. The “550” comes from the old military spec, MIL-C-5040H: Type III cord had to break at no less than 550 pounds. That spec was renamed PIA-C-5040 in 1997. Some people wear a bracelet like this because it can be unwoven into about ten feet of cord. That is a handy extra, never a plan.

Don’t

  • Don’t hand a kid the lighter. Melting is an adult job.
  • Don’t twist the middle cords while you thread the buckle.
  • Don’t trust a bracelet to hold weight. It is a craft, not climbing gear.
