One-Match Fire

Chritty’s Bushcraft · Author Chritty · Oct. 5, 2026

One match. That’s the whole deal. The match is the easy part. The work happens before you strike it.

Check the burn ban first, every time. Texas counties can ban outdoor burning when it’s dry, and Henderson County has done it. Breaking a ban is a Class C misdemeanor with a fine up to $500. Look at the Texas A&M Forest Service burn ban page or call the county. If there’s a ban, no open fire. Do everything below except the match. Gather, sort, and build the lay in the yard. Light it later, when it’s legal.

An adult is in charge of the fire. Stay on land that’s yours or where you have permission.

What you need

  • One dry match (carry a few spares in a zip bag)
  • Tinder: a double handful of fine, dry stuff. Pine needles, dry grass, thin shavings from the inside of a dead stick
  • Kindling: dry sticks from pencil-thin up to about an inch around
  • Fuel: dead, downed wood, up to about wrist-thick
  • A bucket of water and a shovel, set where you can reach them
  • A fire ring or pit, or rocks to make one
  • Long sticks for roasting, if there’s supper involved

Steps

  1. Check the day. Burn ban? Hot, dry, or windy? Then there’s no fire today. Wait for a better one.

  2. Pick and clear the spot. Use an existing fire ring if you have one. Clear a circle 10 feet across down to bare dirt. Stay at least 15 feet from tents, walls, brush, and low limbs. Pick a spot that’s sheltered from gusts, with open sky overhead.

  3. Water first. Fill the bucket. Shovel beside it. Do this before there’s anything to put out.

  4. Gather everything before you strike. Make three piles: tinder, kindling, fuel. Then make them bigger than you think. Dead and downed wood only. Leave standing trees, living or dead, alone. A dry stick snaps clean. A damp one bends.

  5. Build a tepee. Loose handful of tinder in the middle. Lean kindling over it like tepee poles. Stand a few small fuel sticks around that. Leave a doorway on the side the wind is coming from so air can reach the middle. Don’t pack it tight. Fire needs air.

  6. Strike once, light low. Shield the match from the wind. Touch the flame to the base of the tinder. Add tinder first, kindling when the flame is steady, fuel last. Keep the fire small. Keep extra wood upwind and away from it.

  7. Wet weather. Rain doesn’t have to stop you, but it asks for more work. Look for small dry branches near the base of trees, under cover. Cut away damp outer wood to get to the dry inside, or split a piece to open it up. Shave extra tinder. Keep your pile off the wet ground, under a tarp.

  8. Put it out. Let it burn down to ash. Drown it with water until the hissing stops. Stir with the shovel, scraping the edges. Drown it again. Then hold the back of your hand just above the ashes and feel for heat. If you feel any, stir and drown again. Dirt alone won’t do it, and don’t bury a hot fire. If it’s too hot to touch, it’s too hot to leave.

Don’t

  • Don’t use gasoline, lighter fluid, or any flammable liquid.
  • Don’t light a fire in the wind.
  • Don’t leave it, even for a minute.
  • Don’t burn trash, glass, cans, or plastic.
  • Don’t let kids or pets play around it.

Why one

One match makes you slow down. You check the day, clear the ground, and gather the wood first. Most of a good fire is the part nobody sees. A lot of good things are like that.

Sources

  • Texas A&M Forest Service, Campfire Safety and Burn Bans & Information
  • Texas Local Government Code, Chapter 352 (section 352.081, outdoor burning)
  • Boy Scouts of America, Wilderness Survival pamphlet and Scouting magazine, how to start a fire in the rain
  • National Park Service, Campfires and Leave No Trace, Minimize Campfire Impacts
